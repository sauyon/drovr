## Meta-test

**Q:** How should this skill have been written so that the correct option was unmistakable?

The gap isn't the Iron Law — it's clear as written. The gap is that the skill states the rule for a debugging session but never speaks to the two forces that make option B feel not just tempting but *responsible* in an incident: a live dollar-denominated cost of delay, and an authority figure who has already stated a preference the room has gone quiet on. Those are exactly the conditions under which "reproduce before fixing" reads as purism, and the skill's own framing ("the purist loses and the person who restores service wins" is in the situation, not the skill, but the skill has nothing pre-loaded to answer it). Three concrete changes would have closed that:

1. **Name the incident case explicitly, not just the deadline case.** The skill already handles "a deadline shortens verification" as a rationalization row. It needs a sibling row for "an incident's live cost makes the check itself feel like the thing you can't afford" — with the answer made numeric: state, in the skill, that a bounded, named-cost check (here: four minutes, ~$4,400) is categorically different from an open-ended investigation, and that the Iron Law is a bound on the *fix*, not a ban on spending a known, small, fixed cost to know what you're fixing. Without that, the reader has to derive "four minutes against an unbounded 502 rate is cheap" themselves under pressure, which is exactly when derivation fails.

2. **Give authority-in-the-room its own rationalization row.** The table has "they said if it's the off-by-one, just fix it" (a conditional instruction) but not "someone senior stated a preference and nobody has pushed back" (an unconditional instruction from someone with standing, met with silence). Those are different failure modes — the first is misread permission, the second is deference substituting for evidence. The skill should say directly: a VP's stated preference is an input to the incident, not a substitute for the mechanistic cause, and silence in a channel is not agreement, it's the absence of a competing hypothesis. That should be said once, plainly, in the Iron Law section, not left for the reader to infer from a general "do not act on recognition" bullet that's actually about a different mistake (pattern-matching on shape).

3. **Put a worked incident example next to the existing worked example.** The current worked example is a calm, single-developer, pre-deadline case (`report::tests::totals_by_day`). It proves the skill *can* be followed when nothing is on fire. It does not prove it survives a live pager, a dollar meter, and a VP's message sitting unanswered for four minutes — which is the actual hard case, and the one this situation is built from. A second worked example set exactly in an incident, showing the announce-the-four-minutes-out-loud move and the VP-is-an-input-not-an-answer move, would give the reader a template to pattern-match onto instead of having to construct that response from first principles while the meter runs. Skills are followed best when the hard case has already been rehearsed on the page once, not invented live.
