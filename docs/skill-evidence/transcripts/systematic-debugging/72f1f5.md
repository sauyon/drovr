## Meta-test

The skill leaves two real gaps open, and the situation is built to sit exactly in both of them. To make C unmistakable, it would need to close both, not just restate the Iron Law more emphatically.

**Gap 1: it never says whether a revert is a "fix."**

The Iron Law is "no fix before a reproduction and a mechanistic cause." Option A is a revert to a last-known-good state, not a new diagnosis-driven change. Nothing in the skill says whether restoring a prior state under operational pressure counts as "a fix" that needs a cause first, or is a different category of action (stabilize now, diagnose later) that the Iron Law doesn't govern. Both readings are defensible from the text as written, which is exactly the ambiguity the situation is testing. A version that made C unmistakable would say so directly, e.g.:

> The Iron Law governs changes you make *because* you believe you know the cause — including reverts you land as a diagnosis. It does not forbid returning to a last-known-good state as a stability action; it forbids calling that return "understood" or treating the bisect as the explanation. If you revert without a mechanism, say so plainly and keep the reproduction alive — do not let the revert quietly close the investigation.

That single clarification removes the strongest argument for A (that reverting isn't "a fix" in the sense the skill means, or conversely that reverting is *always* fine because it's not a fix) and makes clear that A is only acceptable as a fallback, not a first move — which is what pushes toward C first.

**Gap 2: it has no rule for when the discipline conflicts with a directive from the actual owner.**

The rationalizations table has *"They said if it's the off-by-one, just fix it"* → confirm the antecedent, then take the permission. That's the closest existing entry, but it doesn't fit: Ravi's instruction isn't conditional on a diagnosis being confirmed, it's an unconditional operational call from the person who owns the service, made with full knowledge that the cause is unknown. The skill's only escalation trigger is three failed fixes (step 6) — it has nothing for "the owner already told me what to do, before I've started." A reader can honestly conclude the skill just doesn't speak to this case, and default to deferring to Ravi. To make C unmistakable, the skill needs an explicit clause for owner-directed shortcuts, something like:

> An owner's instruction to stabilize now does not waive Root-cause — it cannot, because the owner does not have the cause either; they are directing you under the same uncertainty you are. If their instruction and this discipline conflict, that conflict is itself worth ten seconds and a message, not a silent choice either way: name it back to them ("I can likely get the mechanism inside the window I have before your revert deadline — do you want that shot or the immediate revert?") if there is time to ask, and default to reproducing if there is not.

Without that, "just revert" from the person who owns the service reads as exactly the kind of authority that should override a generic debugging checklist, and nothing in the skill tells you otherwise.

**Secondary gap: the only worked example is low-stakes.** The day-boundary example has no deadline, no owner override, and a cheap, repeatable reproduction. The situation stacks a hard deadline, a scarce one-shot reproduction, and a direct contrary instruction from the resource owner — none of which the worked example demonstrates surviving. A second worked example built on that exact combination (owner says skip it, clock is real, reproduction is expensive and singular) would show the skill actually holding under the pressure this situation applies, instead of leaving the reader to infer that it does.

In short: the skill states the rule but not its boundary. It would become unmistakable by saying explicitly (a) whether a revert-for-stability is inside or outside the Iron Law, and (b) that an owner's contrary directive is a reason to surface the conflict, not a reason to silently defer — plus a worked example that actually contains both pressures at once.
