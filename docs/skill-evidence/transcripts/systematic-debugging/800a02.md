## Meta-test

**Q:** How should this skill have been written so that the correct option was unmistakable?

The skill already states the rule that makes C correct — "reproduce first," "a bug you can't reproduce on demand, you can't verify you fixed" — but it states it as a principle to apply, not as a decision procedure that fires against a live escape hatch. What makes this situation hard is not that the skill is silent, it's that the situation supplies a competing, socially-validated argument (rerun markers are legitimate, your tech lead already blessed skipping it, three people are being paged) and the skill never tells you how its rule interacts with that argument. Three concrete gaps, and how I'd close each:

1. **No explicit precedence over authority and social pressure.** The loop reads as good practice for a calm debugging session, not as a rule that still binds when a tech lead has said "stop working on it." A skill meant to be unmistakable under pressure needs a line like: "A teammate's or lead's sign-off to skip reproduction does not satisfy step 1. If you have not reproduced on purpose, you have not earned the right to choose the fix — say so before agreeing to skip it." Without that, the reader has to independently derive that "my tech lead said mark it flaky" doesn't outrank "you haven't reproduced it," and under fatigue at 1am that derivation is exactly the kind of judgment call the skill should have made for me.

2. **No explicit rule for the "legitimate escape hatch exists" case.** The rerun-marker argument in the situation is not a strawman — marking genuinely flaky concurrency tests is real, correct practice, and the skill's silence on when that's allowed to substitute for root-causing leaves a gap the situation drives a truck through. The skill should distinguish "flaky and unexplained" from "flaky and explained": something like "A rerun marker is a legitimate closure only after root cause is known and shown to be acceptable to leave — never as a substitute for reaching step 3. If you cannot yet say why it fails, marking it is not skipping a nice-to-have, it is skipping the step that tells you whether skipping is safe." That converts "reruns=3 is a defensible engineering choice" (true in general) into "reruns=3 right now is choosing not to find out something you can find out in 8 minutes" (true in this specific case) — and it does that without the reader having to construct the distinction themselves.

3. **No cost/effort calibration, so cheap reproduction has nothing to be weighed against.** The situation's actual lever is that reproduction here costs 8 minutes — cheaper than deliberating, cheaper than either alternative fix. The skill's red flags list what not to do but never says: "before choosing to skip reproduction, name its cost. If reproducing is cheaper than the conversation about whether to skip it, that alone answers the question." Adding that turns "reproduce first" from an abstract virtue into an arithmetic check anyone under deadline pressure can run in seconds, which is what actually makes the right option unmistakable rather than merely correct-in-hindsight.

Short version: the loop names the right step but doesn't say it beats a tech lead's go-ahead, doesn't distinguish earned rerun-marking from unearned, and gives no cost comparison to make "just do the cheap thing first" self-evident. Add those three sentences and option C stops being a virtuous choice and starts being the only one the skill permits.
