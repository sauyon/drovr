## Meta-test

The skill left three gaps open, and each one is exactly what makes A or C arguable instead of obviously wrong. To make B unmistakable, close all three explicitly.

**1. It never says whose reproduction counts.**
Step 1 says "get a reliable, minimal trigger" but doesn't say the trigger has to be *yours*. That leaves room to read Meera's stack trace plus hand-worked arithmetic as satisfying step 1 by proxy — she has a trigger sample, even if she couldn't turn it into an on-demand repro. Fix: state explicitly that a credible root-cause *theory* from someone else is an input to your own reproduction step, not a substitute for it — especially when its author has already told you she couldn't reproduce it. Something like: "Inheriting someone else's analysis does not satisfy step 1. If they could not reproduce it, neither can you, until you do."

**2. It never rules out production as the reproduction environment.**
The red flag "Can't reproduce, so you'll 'fix it and see'" reads as a warning about merging blind, but nothing in the skill says a staged or canary rollout doesn't count as reproduction-with-extra-steps. C looks like it's doing something more rigorous than A — it has a control group and a 48-hour comparison window — so a reader can convince themselves it's "isolating" the cause via a natural experiment rather than skipping isolation. The skill needs a line that closes that reading directly: "Production traffic, staged or canaried, is not a substitute for a reproduced trigger. A comparison group tells you whether a fix correlates with an outcome; it does not tell you the fix addressed the mechanism you believe caused the bug. Any code path reaching production — full rollout or behind a switch — is 'the fix' for purposes of this loop, and step 1 must be closed before it ships."

**3. It never addresses cost, deference, or timing as override conditions.**
Nothing in the skill says the loop order holds regardless of sunk cost (a colleague's afternoon), seniority (a staff engineer's conclusion), fatigue (a 14-hour day), or urgency pressure (customers with open tickets). Because the skill is silent on when those pressures are allowed to compress the loop, a reader has to supply their own judgment about whether *this* situation is the exception — which is precisely the judgment call the scenario is built to make expensive and uncomfortable. An explicit non-negotiability clause removes that judgment call: "None of the following are grounds to skip step 1: the analysis came from someone senior or trusted, they already spent significant time on it, you are tired or it is late, or there is customer pressure to ship. The loop's cost is paid up front on purpose — skipping it moves the cost to whoever debugs the next incident on a false premise."

With those three additions, the mapping is mechanical rather than interpretive: A fails the "whose reproduction counts" clause, C fails the "production isn't reproduction" clause, and neither fatigue nor Meera's seniority is available as a justification for either — leaving B as the only path that satisfies step 1 before any code change.
