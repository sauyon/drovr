## Meta-test

The skill's Iron Law and rationalization table are written from the point of view of the debugger who *did the analysis themselves*. Every rationalization row assumes the pressure is internal: your own pattern recognition ("we've seen this before"), your own inability to reproduce ("I'll fix it and see"), your own clock, your own streak of failed fixes. The situation doesn't apply pressure through any of those channels. It applies pressure through a channel the skill never names: **someone else's finished, credible work, handed to you.** That gap is what makes A and C arguable instead of unmistakable. Three additions would close it.

**1. State explicitly that the Iron Law is indifferent to authorship and seniority.**

Nothing in the skill says a hand-derived mechanism stops being a hypothesis when a staff engineer writes it instead of you, or when it arrives with a fix and a regression test already attached. A reader can construct a plausible-sounding exception: "the reproduction requirement exists because *my* reasoning might be wrong — but this is Meera's reasoning, and she's the one who owns the service." The skill needs a line closing that off directly, something like:

> A mechanism someone else derived by reading the code — however senior, however careful, however far it goes toward a fix — is still a hypothesis until it produces the failure on demand. Authorship is not evidence. Reproduce it yourself, or watch it reproduced, before it counts as a cause.

**2. Add a rationalization-table row for the handoff case specifically.**

The closest existing row is *"I can't reproduce it, so I'll fix it and see"* — but that's written for the person who *couldn't* reproduce it, not for the person on the receiving end of someone else's honest admission that they couldn't either. Meera's note — "I could not get it to reproduce locally, but reading the code this is the only path" — is the exact sentence the current table has no row for. Add:

| *"A senior engineer already did the analysis and the fix and test are ready to merge — redoing the reproduction is second-guessing them."* | Reproduce it anyway, and say so to them plainly. Their effort and seniority are reasons to trust the fix will confirm, not a reason to skip confirming it. |

Without this row, "their work already satisfies the spirit of the rule" is left for the reader to invent, and the scenario is built so that inventing it is socially cheap (nobody push backs) and emotionally expensive to refuse (calling it out in front of the thread).

**3. Close the staged-rollout loophole that makes C look like caution rather than a guess.**

The Iron Law says "no fix before a reproduction," but option C doesn't feel like *shipping* a fix — it feels like a controlled experiment, gated to three customers, measured for 48 hours. That framing is exactly what makes it attractive: it borrows the vocabulary of rigor (staged, compared, time-boxed) without paying the cost of rigor (an on-demand trigger, a stated mechanism). The skill's "Verify" step already says a production metric trend isn't a substitute for re-running the trigger, but it says this about *verifying a fix already believed correct*, not about *using production as the reproduction environment in the first place*. Add a line making the equivalence explicit:

> A canary, feature flag, or per-customer rollout of an unconfirmed fix is still "acting before a cause" — it only spreads the guess across a slower, noisier instrument. If the plan for finding out whether the fix works is watching a production number move, you have swapped the harness you didn't want to build for one you can't control, staffed by your customers.

**Why these three, and not a rewrite of the Iron Law itself.** The Iron Law's wording ("NO FIX BEFORE A REPRODUCTION AND A MECHANISTIC CAUSE") is already unambiguous on its face — B is the only option that satisfies it as stated. The scenario doesn't work by contradicting the rule, it works by supplying a reader with unaddressed reasons to believe the rule is *already satisfied* (a staff engineer's hand-worked analysis feels like a cause) or *doesn't apply yet* (a canary feels like observation, not a fix). Naming authorship-doesn't-count and staged-rollout-still-counts as explicit cases — the way the table already names "off-by-one, just fix it" and "three fixes in, the fourth will land" — is what would have made the gap between A/C and B disappear before the situation ever supplied its pressure.
