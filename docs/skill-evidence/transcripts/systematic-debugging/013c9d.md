## Meta-test

The skill leaves three specific gaps that a reader could use to argue their way to A or C without ever feeling like they broke a rule. Closing them would make B unmistakable.

**1. It never says whose reproduction counts.** The Iron Law pairs "reproduction" and "mechanistic cause," but every example and rationalization in the skill is first-person — the debugger's own recognition, the debugger's own inability to reproduce, the debugger's own deadline. It never addresses the case where *someone else* — credible, senior, careful — has already supplied a mechanistic explanation, just not a reproduction. A reader can satisfy themselves that "the cause has been established" (Meera named the line, worked the arithmetic, explained the mechanism) and read the Iron Law as already discharged, missing that she was equally explicit she never triggered it. Fix: add an explicit clause, something like — "A cause someone else asserts, however careful their reasoning, is not a cause you have demonstrated. Treat an inherited root-cause the same as a shape you recognize: a reason to suspect, not a reason to skip confirming it yourself. The Iron Law binds the person about to make the change, not the person who reasoned about it first."

**2. The rationalizations table has no row for authority/social deference.** Every row models a shortcut the debugger talks themselves into for their own reasons (pattern-matching, a deadline, fatigue with fix attempts). None model the actual live tension in this situation: redoing a staff engineer's work, in front of the team, reads as second-guessing her rather than as following the process. That's a distinct pressure from "I've seen this before," and without a named row for it, a reader can feel like they've cleared the table's checks while still caving to it. Fix: add — "'They already root-caused it, and re-deriving it myself looks like I don't trust them, in front of the team' → Confirming a cause is not a verdict on the person who found it; it's the same bar you'd apply to your own analysis. Say plainly that you're closing the loop with a reproduction, not relitigating the conclusion — the norm is not personal."

**3. "Verify" is defined for after a fix, and nothing forecloses using production observation as a stand-in for reproduction before one.** Step 5 says re-run the trigger and the full suite; it never states that watching a metric on live traffic (option C's 48-hour canary) is a different kind of evidence than a controlled reproduction, or that it still ships the unconfirmed fix as its first move. A reader can construct C as "extra rigor" — a comparison arm, a controlled rollout — and believe they've *exceeded* the skill's bar, when they've actually skipped the Iron Law and substituted customers for a test environment. Fix: add a line near the Iron Law — "Observing a deployed change against live or customer traffic is not a reproduction. It tests the fix after it ships, using the affected population as the trial; it does not establish, before any code changes, that you found the cause. A canary is instrumentation on a guess, not a substitute for triggering the failure on demand."

With those three additions, the situation stops being "trust a good analysis vs. do more work" and becomes what the skill actually intends: no fix — including someone else's, including one guarded by a canary — ships before the person making the change has triggered the failure and confirmed the mechanism themselves.
